# Ben Day

A Ben Day dot is a printed dot that is always the same size, laid down in a regular pattern so that one ink makes a tint, and it is the texture every color comic from the 1890s to the 1980s was printed in.

The dot is named for the man who sold the film that made it, and the film is a hundred and forty-seven years old. In between, a Sunday newspaper learned to print in color, a comic book learned to show sixty-four colors with four inks on the cheapest paper there is, a painter made the dot the subject of a canvas thirteen feet wide, and a generation of manga artists rubbed it onto paper from a sheet. Then the scanner arrived, and every one of those pages became a file, and the file became a problem: a grid of dots photographed onto a grid of pixels, shown on a third grid of lights, interferes with itself. The word for that interference is older than any of this and is not a man's name. Fuji shows these files, so this page is the history of the dot, the history of the word, and then exactly what Fuji does to the pixels of a scanned page today, named by algorithm, by pass, by operating system call and by CSS rule, since a reader who knows what a viewer does with their pixels can decide whether to trust it.

## The dot

### A printer's son

[Benjamin Henry Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Henry_Day) was born in Springfield, Massachusetts, on April 10, 1810, and on September 3, 1833, in New York, he printed the first issue of *The Sun* and sold it for one cent, when the other papers in the city cost five or six. It was the first penny paper in the United States, it paid for itself with advertising rather than subscriptions, it ran the police court and the Great Moon Hoax of 1835, and he sold it to his brother-in-law Moses Yale Beach in 1838 for forty thousand dollars. He had built the first mass-market newspaper, and the mass market would need pictures.

His son [Benjamin Henry Day Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Henry_Day_Jr.) was born in New York City on March 7, 1838, the year the paper was sold. He studied in Paris, came home, and drew for *Frank Leslie's*, *Harper's Weekly* and *Vanity Fair*, the illustrated weeklies that were the picture press of the 1860s and 1870s. He drew more than twenty of the pictures in Mark Twain's *A Tramp Abroad* of 1880. He was a working illustrator rather than a famous one, and what he knew from the inside was how long it took to shade a drawing by hand: every tint on a lithographic stone was stippled dot by dot or ruled line by line by a craftsman with a pen.

On January 4, 1878, from West Hoboken, New Jersey, he filed for a patent on a way around that, and on April 22, 1879, the Patent Office granted him [U.S. Patent 214,493, "Improvement in Printing-Films"](https://patents.google.com/patent/US214493A/en). The first claim is six words long: "A transparent tinting or printing film." The word dot appears nowhere in it. The text speaks of "light or heavy, straight, swelled, or wavy lines, stipples, or grains," and the stipple, a field of small marks, is the dot's first name.

### The film

[Andy Bleck's history of the Ben Day dot](https://legionofandy.com/2015/09/09/ben-day-dots-part-4-pre-history-origins/), the most thorough account anyone has written, reads the patent and the catalogues closely, and the mechanism is simple. Lines or stipples are engraved on a block, and an impression of the block is taken in tin foil. Warm gelatine mixed with glycerine is poured over the foil and sets into a sheet that is nearly transparent and carries the engraved pattern in relief. The sheet is backed with fine silk, later with celluloid, and sewn onto a wooden frame, and the printers called the framed sheet a screen. To use it, the lithographer inked the raised pattern with a roller, masked the parts of the stone that were to stay clear with gum, lowered the screen over the drawing on its hinged frame, and rubbed the back of the film with a stylus wherever the tint was wanted. The pattern transferred to the stone in greasy ink, and the stone printed it as a tint.

Day's [adjustable frame of 1881](https://legionofandy.com/2015/10/16/ben-day-dots-part-5/) added screws that moved the screen a thirty-second of an inch per turn, so the same pattern could be printed a second time slightly offset and a tint could be doubled or darkened. By 1887 an advertisement listed more than a hundred screens, and a catalogue of 1911 shows a hundred and seventy-two, twenty-six of them stipples. Chromolithography, the color printing of the nineteenth century, took the method up first: *The Times* wrote in 1912 that after 1885 hand stippling had largely been replaced by mechanical stippling, and that it had cheapened the publication of color work. The company held patents in the United States, Canada, Great Britain, France, Germany and Belgium, and printed its instructions in English, French, German, Italian and Spanish. A British artist writing in 1941 still called the best films "Day's medium," after the man.

### Halftone, which is the other dot

The Ben Day dot has a sibling that is older by a few years in idea and younger by a few in practice, and the two are confused constantly. A Ben Day dot is the same size everywhere and makes one flat tint; an artist chooses where it goes. A halftone dot changes size from place to place to follow the tones of a photograph; a camera chooses. [Halftone's history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halftone) runs beside Day's. William Fox Talbot suggested a screen of gauze over a photographic plate in 1852. Stephen Horgan printed the first photograph in a newspaper, of Steinway Hall, in the *New York Daily Graphic* on December 2, 1873, and the first full-tone halftone in an American newspaper, *A Scene in Shantytown*, in the same paper on March 4, 1880. Frederic Ives of Philadelphia patented the first commercially successful process in 1881, Georg Meisenbach patented his *Autotypie* in Germany in 1882, and Max Levy's ruled glass screen of 1893 made the crossline screen a product a shop could buy. *Scientific American* printed a halftone of Alexander Graham Bell on November 5, 1892, and [the wood engraving trade was gone by the end of that decade](https://exhibits.library.cornell.edu/DawnsEarlyLight/feature/the-rise-of-mass-media). From then on a photograph went into print as a grid of dots, and so did everything the newspapers printed in color.

### The Sunday paper and the Yellow Kid

Color came to the American newspaper in 1892, when the *Chicago Inter-Ocean* printed the first Sunday color section in the country on a press built to copy Hippolyte Marinoni's French color rotary, and New York's *World*, *Herald* and *Journal* followed with color pages from 1893. [Bleck's chapter on the letterpress 1890s](https://legionofandy.com/2016/03/04/the-history-of-ben-day-dots-part-6-letterpress-printing-1890s-early-sunday-comics/) follows the dot into those pages. Richard Outcault's *Hogan's Alley* first ran in color in May 1895, at least partly tinted with Ben Day screens, and *The Yellow Kid and his New Phonograph*, printed in the *New York Journal* on October 25, 1896, is the page usually named as the first comic strip. In letterpress the method changed: the engraver masked the zinc plate for each color, pressed the inked screen's dots onto it, and etched away everything that was not a dot or a solid, so each plate printed its ink as a field of identical dots wherever the artist had asked for a tint. The Sunday comics were born in Ben Day, and the comic book inherited the whole apparatus.

### Four inks, sixty-four colors

The comic book of the 1950s and 1960s was printed on newsprint by [four-color rotary letterpress](https://legionofandy.com/2021/04/06/ben-day-dots-part-9-1950s-to-1980s/), most of it at [World Color Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worldcolor), a St. Louis company founded in 1903 to print color for the 1904 World's Fair, whose plant at Sparta, Illinois, opened in 1948 and by the early 1980s printed nearly every comic book in America. The colors were chosen by a colorist, who painted a photostat of the inked page with dyes and then wrote a code beside each area for the separator. The code was not CMYK. It was YRB, yellow, red and blue, with a number for the tint: a bare letter was solid ink, a 2 was a 25 percent tint, a 3 was 50 percent, and a code like YR2B3 stacked solid yellow, a quarter red and half blue. Three tints of three inks, with black for the lines, gave sixty-three colors and the white of the paper, which is the sixty-four-color palette the era is remembered for. The separations were made by hand, mostly by women, on painted acetate overlays at the Chemical Color Plate Corporation in Bridgeport, Connecticut, which did the work for Marvel, DC and Archie, and the tints were put on the plates through contact screens of sixty lines to the inch, with each color's screen turned thirty degrees from the last.

By then Day's gelatine film had almost entirely gone out of use. What survived was the look, a field of identical dots at one of three densities, and the name. Every reader of a comic book knew the dots without knowing they had a name, until a painter made them impossible to miss.

### Lichtenstein

In 1961 Roy Lichtenstein painted [*Look Mickey*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Look_Mickey), four feet by nearly six, from an illustration in the 1960 Disney storybook *Donald Duck Lost and Found*, after his son pointed at a comic and bet that he could not paint as well as that. In 1963 he painted [*Whaam!*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whaam!), a diptych thirteen feet wide, from a panel Irv Novick drew for *All-American Men of War* number 89 of February 1962, and [*Drowning Girl*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drowning_Girl), from Tony Abruzzo's splash page for "Run for Love!" in *Secret Hearts* number 83. The Tate bought *Whaam!* in 1966 and the Museum of Modern Art bought *Drowning Girl* in 1971. He painted the dots through a handmade metal screen with a scrub brush, and by 1963 had settled on a stencil that gave them a mechanical regularity the comics themselves never quite had. The dots were the argument of the paintings: a painter's hand pretending to be a press, at a size where nobody could pretend the dots were anything else. After him the Ben Day dot was a sign, and anything printed with it read as a comic even when it was a poster, a shirt or an animated film.

### Screentone

The dot moved from the printer's shop to the artist's desk as a sheet of adhesive film, which is Day's screen turned inside out: the pattern is already printed, and the artist cuts it and burnishes it onto the drawing. [Zip-A-Tone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screentone) claims first use in 1937, Chart-Pak followed in 1949, and Letraset sold the same thing as [Letratone from 1966](https://downthetubes.net/who-remembers-letratone/). A sheet has a clear backing, the printed pattern, and a layer of wax; the artist lays it face down, rubs the back with a stylus, and peels the backing away, and the pattern stays where it was rubbed. Scraped with a blade, it makes a starburst or a cloud. Western comics used it for texture and shading, and Japanese manga, printed in black on cheap paper with no color at all, built its whole language of tone on it: skies, blushes, speed, the gray of a school uniform, all screentone. A manga page of the 1980s is a Ben Day page with the color taken out, and a scanned one has the same dots at the same densities.

### The word that is not a man

Moiré is the pattern that appears when two regular patterns are laid over each other, and the word is French for a watered silk. [The fabric came first](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moir%C3%A9_pattern): silk makers pressed two wet layers of ribbed cloth together, the ribs did not quite line up, and when the cloth dried it carried a rippled sheen that looks like water. The French noun *moire* is a seventeenth-century borrowing of the English word mohair, attested in 1610, which the English had from the French *mocayart*, the Italians from *mocaiarro*, and everyone from [the Arabic *mukhayyar*](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/mohair), the chosen cloth, goat's hair of the best quality. The adjective *moiré*, watered, is attested from 1823. So the word is a cloth, then a finish, then the look of that finish, and only then the effect that gives a photograph of a screen door its ripples. Nobody named Moiré invented anything.

The effect is a beat. Two gratings of nearly the same pitch, laid together, go in and out of step, and the eye sees the slow pattern where they drift: for two sets of lines of spacing *p* and *p* + δ*p*, the bands fall about *p*²/δ*p* apart, and for two identical gratings turned by a small angle α they fall about *p*/α apart. Four halftone screens printed on one page are four gratings, and [the printer's whole craft of screen angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moir%C3%A9_pattern) exists to turn the beat between them into a pattern too fine and too even to notice, the rosette, by keeping the angles thirty degrees apart. A screen out of angle shows the beat as a plaid across a face. That is the moiré the comic separators of the 1960s guarded against, and the one that arrived on every printed page when the mid-1970s camera systems put every color at the same angle.

The third grating is the one this page is about. A scanner samples a printed page on a grid of its own, and a screen of sixty dots to the inch sampled at three hundred dots to the inch is five samples per dot, which is enough; sampled at seventy-two, it is barely one, and the dots fold into a slow beat with the sampling grid. Signal processing calls that aliasing, and the halftone screen is the textbook case of a signal above the Nyquist frequency of the sampler. The same thing happens again, in software, every time the scan is shown smaller than it was scanned: shrinking a scan to fit a window is sampling it again, on the grid of the window, and a method that shrinks by picking pixels rather than averaging them makes the beat on the screen rather than on paper. Scanner software calls the cure descreening, and the reliable form of it is to scan high, blur the dots away, and shrink. Fuji does not descreen anything. What it does is choose, at every place it shrinks or enlarges a picture, a method that averages rather than picks, and the next section is which method, where.

## What Fuji does to the pixels

Four pixel units are at work in everything below, and the thumbnails page measured what happens when two of them are confused. **Image pixels** are the picture's own, the grid the scanner or the camera made. **CSS pixels** are the page's and the user's: what a thumbnail is sized in and what a key on the light table counts in. **Backing pixels** are the grid the web engine paints into, `devicePixelRatio` of them to a CSS pixel, two on a Retina Mac and one and a half on a Windows laptop at 150 percent. **Panel pixels** are the lights, and on a Mac in a scaled mode macOS resamples the whole backing store onto them, invisibly to every program. Fuji's rule, everywhere it can hold, is that a picture is resampled once, from image pixels to backing pixels, by a filter that reads every source pixel, and that the backing pixels then reach the compositor one to one with nothing to do.

### A thumbnail from the operating system

On macOS and Windows a JPEG or a PNG never reaches the web engine at all. Rust opens the file through the operating system's own imaging library, the one Finder and Explorer use for their thumbnails, learns the picture's size and orientation from the header, runs the fit to get a size in CSS pixels, and turns that into backing pixels with the same multiplication and rounding the page will use for the canvas:

```rust
let (css_width, css_height, _) = crate::fit::fit_size(&ask.fit, width, height, ask.beam, ask.screen_width, ask.screen_height)?;
let want_width  = (css_width  as f64 * ask.backing_per_css).round().max(1.0);
let want_height = (css_height as f64 * ask.backing_per_css).round().max(1.0);
```

On the Mac the library is [ImageIO](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/imageio), and one call does the decode, the scale and the orientation together. Three options go in: the longer side in backing pixels, which is the only size ImageIO takes; a flag to render from the picture rather than hand back the 160 by 120 preview a camera embeds; and a flag to apply the EXIF orientation.

```rust
let options: CFDictionary<CFString, CFType> = CFDictionary::from_CFType_pairs(&[
	(key(unsafe { kCGImageSourceThumbnailMaxPixelSize }),          CFNumber::from(maximum as i64).as_CFType()),
	(key(unsafe { kCGImageSourceCreateThumbnailFromImageAlways }), CFBoolean::true_value().as_CFType()),
	(key(unsafe { kCGImageSourceCreateThumbnailWithTransform }),   CFBoolean::true_value().as_CFType()),
]);
let image = unsafe { CGImageSourceCreateThumbnailAtIndex(source, 0, options.as_concrete_TypeRef()) };
```

[`CGImageSourceCreateThumbnailAtIndex`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/imageio/cgimagesourcecreatethumbnailatindex(_:_:_:)) decodes a baseline JPEG at a half, a quarter or an eighth of its size where the codec allows, by leaving out most of the inverse transform, and resamples the rest of the way with a filter that reads every source pixel. The full-size raster never exists. The result is drawn once, one to one, into a Core Graphics bitmap context of the color space the page asked for, sRGB or Display P3, and that draw is where the colors are converted and nothing is resampled.

On Windows the library is the [Windows Imaging Component](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/wic/-wic-about-windows-imaging-codec), and the same work is a chain of objects, each reading from the one before and none of them running until the last asks for pixels. The codec is asked for the nearest size it can decode to natively through [`IWICBitmapSourceTransform`](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/wincodec/nn-wincodec-iwicbitmapsourcetransform), which for a JPEG is the same half, quarter or eighth. The rest of the reduction is the scaler, set to the Fant mode, and the orientation is a flip-rotator, because WIC leaves EXIF to the caller. A profile the file carries is converted to sRGB, and a format converter writes straight-alpha RGBA, top row first.

```rust
transform.GetClosestSize(&mut closest_width, &mut closest_height)?;
scaler.Initialize(&source, want_width, want_height, WICBitmapInterpolationModeFant)?;
rotator.Initialize(&source, transform_for(orientation))?;
converter.Initialize(&source, &GUID_WICPixelFormat32bppRGBA, WICBitmapDitherTypeNone, None, 0.0, WICBitmapPaletteTypeCustom)?;
converter.CopyPixels(std::ptr::null(), stride, &mut pixels)?;
```

Fant is the resampler to know. Microsoft's own description of [`WICBitmapInterpolationModeFant`](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/wincodec/ne-wincodec-wicbitmapinterpolationmode) is one sentence: "Destination pixel values are computed as a weighted average of all the pixels that map to the new pixel." Karl Fant published it in *IEEE Computer Graphics and Applications* in January 1986, "A Nonaliasing, Real-Time Spatial Transform Technique," and Honeywell patented it as [US 4,835,532](https://patents.google.com/patent/US4835532). The title says what it is for: a reduction that integrates every source pixel into each output pixel cannot alias, because no pixel is skipped, and a halftone shrunk through it comes out as the tint the printer meant rather than as a beat. The other modes on that page, nearest neighbor, bilinear and bicubic, read one, four and sixteen source pixels per output pixel whatever the ratio, and at the ratios a thumbnail needs, twenty to one, they read almost nothing. The same holds for ImageIO's thumbnail path, which is why a thumbnail of a scanned comic page on the contact sheet is a smooth tint of the right color.

Either way the thumbnail crosses the bridge as one buffer, twenty bytes of header and then the pixels, and the page puts it onto a canvas with one call.

```js
let context = canvas.getContext('2d', {colorSpace: flowGamut})
context.putImageData(new ImageData(pixels, width, height, {colorSpace: gamut}), 0, 0)
flowEdge(context, canvas, width, height)
```

### A thumbnail from the page

Everything the operating system is not asked for, WebP, AVIF and BMP on Windows, and every raster file on Linux, is decoded by the web engine at full size and shrunk by the page. A single [`drawImage`](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/CanvasRenderingContext2D/drawImage) from a 26-megapixel decode into a small canvas aliases on the Mac, because Core Graphics' interpolation reads a fixed footprint around each output pixel and at twenty-six to one most of the picture is never read. So the page halves, through two scratch canvases taken in turn, until the last draw is within two to one, which is the ratio at which a fixed footprint is a true average of its source. Every step asks for [`imageSmoothingQuality`](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/CanvasRenderingContext2D/imageSmoothingQuality) of `high`, since the default, `low`, reads only the four nearest pixels and would alias even at two to one.

```js
while (size.x > target.x * 2) {
	let half = xy(Math.ceil(size.x / 2), Math.ceil(size.y / 2))
	let canvas = scratch[step % 2]
	canvas.width = half.x; canvas.height = half.y
	let c = canvas.getContext('2d', {colorSpace: flowGamut})
	c.imageSmoothingQuality = 'high'
	c.drawImage(source, 0, 0, half.x, half.y)
	source = canvas; size = half; step++
}
context.imageSmoothingQuality = 'high'
context.drawImage(source, 0, 0, target.x, target.y)
```

A chain of halvings is itself an averaging filter: each step reads every pixel of the one before, so by the last draw every source pixel has contributed to the result. On Chromium `high` is already a chain of halvings under a cubic filter, so on Windows this is the work the engine would have done; on WebKit it is the difference between a smooth tint and jaggies.

### The canvas on the glass

A thumbnail is a `<canvas>` rather than an `<img>` because a canvas is a number of bytes Fuji chose and the engine can never take back, and because its bitmap can be made exactly the size of its box on screen. The engine lays a canvas out on whole CSS pixels and paints it in backing pixels, and a bitmap even one backing pixel short of that box is resampled on every row; the [thumbnails page](./thumbnails.html) has the measurement, in which a test pattern of one-pixel stripes turned to flat gray through the middle of half of all thumbnails on a Retina Mac. So the canvas is sized to the box, in backing pixels, and the picture is put in its corner.

```js
function flowSnap(side, have) {
	let backingPerCss = window.devicePixelRatio
	let want = Math.round(side * backingPerCss)
	return want > have + backingPerCss ? have : want
}
```

The CSS around it is chosen so that nothing touches the bitmap. The frame around a thumbnail is an outline rather than a border, because a border takes its width out of the box the canvas is sized to and resamples every thumbnail into what is left; an outline is drawn outside the box and takes no room. The rounded corner is a clip the compositor applies, which hides a few pixels in each corner and moves none. The shadow is painted outside the box. And the gamma lens, when it is on, is a filter applied to the finished pixels, and when it is off it is `none`.

```css
.myTile {
	display: block;
	border-radius: 4px;
	outline: 2px solid var(--color-frame);
	outline-offset: 0;
	box-shadow: 4px 4px 12px var(--color-shade);
}
.myTile, .myImage {
	filter: var(--gamma-filter, none);
}
```

One rule in the stylesheet applies to every picture Fuji shows, and it cancels a default. Tailwind's reset gives every `<img>` a `max-width` of 100 percent, which shrinks any picture wider than its container and has the engine resample it without anything in Fuji asking. Fuji turns that off, so that a picture is resized only where Fuji says so:

```css
@layer base {
	img { max-width: none }
}
```

### The light table

The ***Diamond Table*** shows a picture the other way round. The file is read into a blob, an object URL points an `<img>` at it, and [`decode()`](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/HTMLImageElement/decode) produces the full-size frame off the main thread. That one element is put on the card and stays there; a flip hides one element and shows another, and nothing is drawn by hand. The card's size and place are computed in real numbers and snapped to the backing grid before they reach the page, so that a position never falls on a fraction of a backing pixel, which would make the engine resample an edge:

```js
export function xySnap(a, backingPerCss) { return xy(Math.round(a.x * backingPerCss) / backingPerCss, Math.round(a.y * backingPerCss) / backingPerCss) }
```

```js
let backingPerCss = window.devicePixelRatio
let scale = quiverA.diamond / (quiverA.natural.x + quiverA.natural.y)
quiverB.space = xySnap(quiverA.space, backingPerCss)
quiverB.card2 = xySnap(xy(quiverA.natural, '*', scale), backingPerCss)
quiverB.card1 = xySnap(xy(quiverB.space, '-', xy(quiverB.card2, '/', 2)), backingPerCss)
```

The picture fills the card, and the card is the picture's own shape, so [`object-fit`](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/object-fit) of `fill` stretches nothing:

```css
.myCard :deep(.myImage) {
	position: absolute;
	top: 0; left: 0; width: 100%; height: 100%;
	object-fit: fill;
	display: none;
}
```

The engine then resamples the decoded frame once, from image pixels to the card's backing pixels, with the filter it uses for any `<img>` that sets no [`image-rendering`](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/image-rendering), and the result is composited one to one because every edge of the card is on the grid. Nothing in Fuji sets `image-rendering` unless the `R` key below is on, so a picture enlarged on the light table is smoothed. What the engine's filter does at a large reduction is not documented by either engine and has not been measured on the light table; the [thumbnails page](./thumbnails.html) found that Core Graphics reads a fixed footprint of source pixels around each output pixel, the reading that made a one-pass `drawImage` alias on the Mac, and the same drawing code paints an `<img>`.

The number keys are the one place the light table promises an exact ratio. `1` to `6` set the card to exactly that many CSS pixels per image pixel:

```js
function zoomNatural(n) {
	zoom(n * (quiverA.natural.x + quiverA.natural.y), xy(frameSize(), '/', 2))
}
```

A whole number of CSS pixels per image pixel is a whole number of backing pixels per image pixel wherever `devicePixelRatio` is whole, so on an ordinary monitor `1` puts one image pixel on one backing pixel and `2` puts it on a two-by-two block, and on a Retina Mac `1` is already a two-by-two block. At those ratios a bilinear filter has nothing to interpolate between on the pixel centers and the picture comes out as blocks with softened edges; at every other zoom, the wheel's steps and a right drag, the ratio is a real number and every row is a blend of two source rows.

### What that means for a page of dots

A scanned comic page or manga page is a picture of a grid, and every path above treats it as a photograph. Reduced to a thumbnail, through ImageIO, through Fant, or through the halving chain, every dot is averaged into its neighbors and the tint comes out as the color the printer intended, which is right for a thumbnail. On the light table at a reduction, the engine's filter reads a footprint of source pixels that does not grow with the ratio, so at two to one it averages and at five to one it can skip rows, and a sixty-line screen scanned at three hundred dots to the inch can show a beat at some sizes and not at others, exactly as the scanner did. On the light table enlarged, the dots are smoothed into soft disks, and a GIF from 1996 that was drawn pixel by pixel is smoothed the same way, which nobody who drew it wanted. Those two cases, the page that needs its dots averaged by a filter wide enough to hold a whole dot, and the picture that needs no filter at all, are the subject of the rest of this page.

## The grid Fuji can choose

Everything above is how Fuji draws a picture unless asked otherwise. What follows is the setting that asks, worked through from the four grids, which choices are Fuji's to make and which belong to the platform. The section after it is the first piece of it that is built.

### Four grids again

A picture on the light table passes through four grids, and the setting touches one of them.

**Image pixels** are the file's. A GIF from 1996 that is 256 pixels across has 256 of them, drawn one at a time by somebody with a mouse, and each one was a decision. A scan of a comic page has a few thousand across, and each one is a sample of paper and ink taken by a scanner, with the dots of the printing screen sampled onto it.

**CSS pixels** are the page's. The card on the light table is sized in them, and the number keys count them: `2` means two CSS pixels per image pixel. They are the unit a user can name, and the unit a setting is written in, and no paint ever happens in them. A CSS pixel is a coordinate, not a place where a color is stored.

**Backing pixels** are where the paint happens. When the engine draws the card it rasterizes the decoded image into a bitmap at the card's size times `devicePixelRatio`, and that bitmap is the only place image pixels become colors on a grid of the screen's. Every choice in this design is a choice about that one draw: which image pixels each backing pixel reads, and with what weights.

**Panel pixels** are the lights. On a Windows machine at its native resolution the backing grid is the panel, and what the engine painted is what the lights show. On a Mac in a scaled mode, which is the mode nearly every Retina Mac runs in, macOS resamples the whole backing store onto the panel afterward, at a ratio like 2560 to 3420, and no program on the machine can see that resample or opt out of it.

So the setting is about the third grid, and the first and the fourth frame it from either side. The user speaks CSS, the engine paints backing, and the panel has the last word.

### The ratio that matters, and who sets each half of it

The number that decides what a picture looks like on the light table is backing pixels per image pixel, and it is a product of two numbers that two different parties set:

<div class="equation">
<math display="block"><mfrac><mtext>backing</mtext><mtext>image</mtext></mfrac><mo>=</mo><mfrac><mtext>CSS</mtext><mtext>image</mtext></mfrac><mo>×</mo><mfrac><mtext>backing</mtext><mtext>CSS</mtext></mfrac></math>
</div>

The first factor is the user's. The number keys set it to a whole number, `F` and `W` set it to whatever fits, and the wheel and a right drag set it to anything at all. The second factor is the platform's: 1 on an ordinary monitor, 2 on a Retina Mac, and 1.25, 1.5 or 1.75 on a Windows laptop at the scales people actually run. Fuji reads it and never sets it.

That product is whole, or it is not, and a rasterization setting only means something clean when it is. A whole number of backing pixels per image pixel means every image pixel can become an exact square block of backing pixels, with no row or column shared between two source pixels. A fraction means some rows are two backing pixels tall and some are one, whatever filter is chosen, and nearest-neighbor on a fractional ratio is a picture with a visible stutter every few rows. The keys are whole in CSS, and that is only the same as whole in backing on some machines:

| key | ordinary monitor, 1 | Windows at 150%, 1.5 | Retina Mac, 2 |
| :-: | --: | --: | --: |
| `1` | 1 | 1.5 | 2 |
| `2` | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| `3` | 3 | 4.5 | 6 |
| `4` | 4 | 6 | 8 |

Backing pixels per image pixel, for each number key. On the ordinary monitor every key is clean. On the Retina Mac every key is clean and there is no key for one image pixel on one backing pixel, which would be half a CSS pixel per image pixel. At 150 percent the odd keys are fractions and the even keys are whole, and `1`, the key that says actual size, is the worst of them. A design that promises perfect pixels has to decide whether a key means a whole number of CSS pixels, as it does today, or a whole number of backing pixels, which on that Windows laptop would make `1` mean two thirds of a CSS pixel and the picture a little smaller than the user asked for, and on a Retina Mac would make `1` mean half a CSS pixel and the picture half the size it is today.

### The panel has the last word

Suppose the product is whole and the engine paints a clean block. On Windows at native resolution the block reaches the lights, and a 256-pixel GIF at `2` is 512 lights across with every edge sharp. On the MacBook Air in its "looks like" 1710 by 1112 mode, the same block is painted into a backing store of 3420 by 2224 and then resampled by macOS onto 2560 by 1664 lights, three panel pixels for every four backing pixels. A two-by-two block becomes a one-and-a-half by one-and-a-half smear, and a four-by-four block becomes three by three with soft edges, and no setting in Fuji can touch it. The only way to a clean block on that machine is a display mode where backing and panel agree, "looks like" 1280 by 832, which is the user's to set in System Settings and not Fuji's.

Fuji can at least say so. It knows the panel's size through the same call that will one day serve the `Ctrl` `0` key, and it can show the panel pixels per image pixel beside the backing pixels on the hud, so that a user who sees soft edges on a Mac knows which of the three grids put them there. That number is read from the main display today, and a window on a second monitor is told about the first; the reading is a start rather than a finish.

### What there is to choose

With the grid chosen, the setting is the filter: how each backing pixel reads the image pixels under it. The platforms offer three levers, and each one is built for a different case.

**One CSS property on the `<img>`.** [`image-rendering`](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/image-rendering) has three values that work. `auto` is what the light table does now, the engine's own smoothing. `pixelated` asks for nearest-neighbor to the nearest whole multiple of the image's size and then smooth scaling to the final size, so a whole ratio is pure blocks and a fractional one is blocks softened at the seams rather than stuttering. `crisp-edges` asks for nearest-neighbor at any ratio and lets the stutter show. This is the lever shaped like the gamma lens: one property, one element, nothing decoded or drawn again, and the animated GIF keeps animating because it stays an `<img>`. It is the lever the `R` key below uses for the enlargement case, and it costs one line. What each engine does with `pixelated` at a fractional ratio has not been measured, and the fractional ratios live on Windows.

**A canvas drawn by the page.** The contact sheet already owns this path: `drawImage` with `imageSmoothingQuality` set, or `imageSmoothingEnabled` turned off for nearest-neighbor, into a canvas sized to the backing grid exactly, as `flowSnap` sizes a thumbnail. On the light table it would mean showing a canvas in the card instead of the store's element, which gives up the animation of a GIF, costs a draw on the main thread at every zoom, and holds a second copy of the picture at the card's size. What it buys is control the CSS property does not have: the page can choose the filter and the ratio together, and it can draw at exactly one backing pixel per image pixel on a Retina Mac, where no key can ask for that today.

**A wider filter for the reduction case.** A page of Ben Day dots shown smaller than it was scanned needs the opposite of nearest-neighbor: a filter wide enough that every backing pixel averages a whole dot or more, so the screen comes out as a tint and never as a beat. CSS has no value for that; `auto` is as smooth as the property gets, and what the engine does at a large reduction is the unmeasured footprint the light table section describes. The page has it already, in the halving chain the thumbnails use, which reads every source pixel by the last draw, and a box filter or a blur before the draw would widen it further. That is a canvas again, and a reduction rather than an enlargement, so it sits on the other side of one to one from the GIF case and may be a different setting rather than the other position of the same toggle.

## Raster in Fuji

Press `R` on the light table and the picture on the card shows its own pixels as blocks. Press it again and the picture is smoothed, as it is at every launch and as it was before the key existed. Nothing is read, decoded or drawn again, the flip is as fast as before, and a GIF keeps animating.

It is a lens over the card, the way gamma is a lens over every view: off at every launch, written nowhere, and separate from the contact sheet, whose thumbnails are reductions and are always averaged. The `I` hud says which it is, `Smooth` or `Rasterized`, on the line that also carries the gamma, and shows the zoom above it in CSS and in backing pixels per picture pixel, so the ratio the blocks depend on is always in view.

What it does depends on the ratio, which the grid section above works through. At a whole number of backing pixels per image pixel, which is every number key on an ordinary monitor and on a Retina Mac, and the even keys on a Windows laptop at 150 percent, each image pixel becomes a block of its own color and nothing is blended: the palette of a 1996 GIF stays its palette, its dither stays a dither, and a one-pixel line is exactly as many backing pixels wide as the key says. `2` on a 1:1:1 machine is four backing pixels per image pixel, each the pixel's own color. Below one to one the same setting drops image pixels rather than averaging them, which on a photograph is sparkle and on a scanned page of dots is moiré; the key means what it says at every ratio, and the hud says the ratio.

### Under the hood

The whole mechanism is one CSS property, one class and one key, and it is written so that the off position cannot differ from the table before it. The property is [`image-rendering`](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/image-rendering), and its value is `pixelated`, the value both engines Fuji ships on support, which asks for nearest-neighbor to the nearest whole multiple of the image's size. The rule lives in the light table's own scoped styles beside the rule that places the picture, and it matches only while the card carries a class:

```css
.myCard :deep(.myImage) {
	position: absolute;
	top: 0; left: 0; width: 100%; height: 100%;
	object-fit: fill;
	display: none;
}
.myCard.myRaster :deep(.myImage) {
	image-rendering: pixelated;
}
```

The first rule is unchanged, so a card without the class is styled exactly as it always was. The `:deep()` is there because the picture is the store's own element, adopted into the card rather than written in the template, and a plain scoped rule could never reach it. The class is bound to one ref, in a module shaped like the gamma module:

```js
export const raster = ref(false)

export function rasterToggle() {
	raster.value = !raster.value
}
```

```html
<div ref="cardRef" class="myCard myShadow myDry myWillChangeTransform bg-well" :class="{myRaster: raster}">
```

And the key is answered on the table, beside `I`, rather than in the shell where `G` is, because gamma is a way of looking at every view at once and this is a way of looking at the card:

```js
else if (!Ctrl && key == 'r') { rasterToggle() }
```

Why this is enough for the enlargement case, and why it is right that it costs nothing: the engine rasterizes the card's picture from image pixels into the card's backing pixels once, and `image-rendering` chooses the filter for that one draw. The card's edges are already snapped to the backing grid, so the layer composites one to one and the blocks reach the backing store intact. The gamma filter applies to the finished pixels afterward, so a pixelated picture through the gamma lens stays pixelated. And because the rule is scoped to the card, no thumbnail can match it: the contact sheet's canvases are in another component with another scope, and the module that holds the ref is imported by the light table alone.
